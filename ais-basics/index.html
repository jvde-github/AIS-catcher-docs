    What is AIS?

    Ships broadcast who they are, where they are and where they are heading. With an antenna and a receiver, AIS-catcher lets you listen in and see the traffic around you.

    Start receiving What you need

    AIS in one minute

    The Automatic Identification System is the radio system ships use to say who and where they are. Every commercial vessel, and many leisure craft, carries a transponder that keeps sending short reports on the marine VHF band. Anyone nearby with a receiver can pick them up, without an internet connection.

    From ship to live map.

    AIS-catcher turns the radio messages your antenna picks up into ships on a map.

    1. 1Ships broadcastShort reports on marine VHF, around 162 MHz
    2. 2Your antenna hearsBest high and outside, with a view of the water
    3. 3AIS-catcher decodesFrom an SDR dongle or a dAISy-catcher, both channels at once
    4. 4You see the shipsOn your own map, or passed on to a chart plotter and your own tools

    More than a position.

    Every message carries the ship's MMSI, a nine-digit number. The rest comes in three kinds of report.

    • Where is the ship?Position, speed and course over ground, heading, rate of turnEvery 2 s to 3 min, by class and speed
    • Which vessel is it?Name, call sign, ship type and dimensionsEvery 6 min
    • Where is it going?Destination, ETA, draught and navigational statusEvery 6 min

    That is why a ship often shows on the map before its name does: the name comes in the static report, sent only every six minutes.

    Four quick facts.

    162 MHzThe marine VHF band
    2 channelsAIS 1 and AIS 2, shared by every ship
    Receive-onlyAIS-catcher never transmits
    Line of sightAntenna height sets the range

    No internet needed to receive. AIS-catcher decodes on your own computer. The internet is only for installing it and for sharing with the community map.

    Want to go deeper?

    None of this is needed before you install.

    How far will you receive?

    VHF signals travel in essentially straight lines, so the curve of the earth sets a hard limit on range: the radio horizon. How far away it lies depends on the height of both antennas.

    For a large ship with its antenna around 15 m above the water, your antenna height gives roughly this range:

    Window sill, 2 m22 km Rooftop, 5 m25 km Mast on the roof, 10 m29 km Apartment block, 20 m34 km Hill or tower, 50 m45 km

    Height beats everything. Raising the antenna does more for your range than any amplifier or better receiver: a clear view of the water matters more than gain.

    Ranges beyond the horizon do occur. In some weather VHF signals bend along the earth's surface (ducting), and ships hundreds of kilometres away show up for a while. See Long Range AIS.

    Antenna and cable

    Any antenna tuned for the marine VHF band works. A simple quarter-wave whip or a dedicated AIS antenna does better than the telescopic antenna that comes with most SDR dongles. What is often overlooked is the cable: coax loses signal quickly at 162 MHz, and every 3 dB of loss halves the power that reaches the receiver.

    CableLoss per 10 m at about 160 MHz
    RG-582.0 dB
    RG-8X1.5 dB
    RG-2130.9 dB
    LMR-4000.5 dB

    A long coax run can be avoided altogether: put the receiver, for example a Raspberry Pi with the SDR dongle, next to the antenna and run a network cable to the house instead. A network cable loses no signal. See What You Need for the parts.

    Time slots and transponder classes

    AIS is a broadcast system: there are no connections and no acknowledgements. Each vessel sends short messages in time slots on two shared channels. Every minute holds 2,250 slots per channel, and the transponders agree among themselves who uses which slot, a scheme called SOTDMA. A receiver simply listens to both channels and decodes every message it hears.

    ClassCarried byPowerPosition reports
    Class ACommercial vessels, required12.5 WEvery 2–10 s under way, every 3 min at anchor
    Class BLeisure craft and small vessels, voluntary2–5 WEvery 30 s under way, every 3 min below 2 knots

    The difference in power is why large ships come in from much further away than small craft.

    The radio channels

    AIS sits just above the marine VHF voice channels, around 162 MHz. Each channel carries 9,600 bit/s, with GMSK modulation.

    ChannelFrequencyUse
    AIS 1 (channel A)161.975 MHzPosition reports and data, ship to ship and ship to shore
    AIS 2 (channel B)162.025 MHzPosition reports and data, ship to ship and ship to shore
    AIS 3 (channel C)156.775 MHzLong-range reports for reception by satellite
    AIS 4 (channel D)156.825 MHzLong-range reports for reception by satellite

    Channels A and B carry nearly all traffic. They are the two AIS-catcher receives at once by default.

    Why an SDR works as an AIS receiver

    A traditional AIS receiver is dedicated hardware. A software-defined radio does the same job in software: the dongle digitizes a slice of the VHF band, and AIS-catcher demodulates and decodes both AIS channels from it at once. It costs a fraction of dedicated equipment, and the same dongle can receive many other signals.

    A dAISy-catcher does the radio work on its own board instead. See which receiver for the difference in practice, and Wikipedia for the history of AIS.

    Receive-only, for hobby and research use. AIS-catcher is not approved for navigation, safety-of-life use or commercial vessel operations: those need type-approved equipment. Read the disclaimer
